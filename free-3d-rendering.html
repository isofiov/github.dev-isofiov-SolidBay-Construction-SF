Why It Matters

A RENDERING CATCHES
PROBLEMS A FLOOR
PLAN CAN'T

A 2D floor plan tells you where a wall or an island goes, but it doesn't tell you how the space actually feels once you're standing in it. We've had clients look at a rendering and realize the island they'd planned would crowd the walkway to the back door, or that a wall of upper cabinets would block a window they didn't want to lose — the kind of thing that's obvious once you can see it, and easy to miss on paper.

Because the rendering is produced by our own in-house design team rather than outsourced, it's built from the same measurements, structural constraints, and material selections that go into your actual permit set and construction drawings — not a generic mockup. What you see in the rendering is what we're pricing and what we're building.

What We Need From You

MAKING A GOOD
RENDERING

The rendering is only as accurate as what we start with. For most requests, a short phone consultation covering rough room dimensions, what's staying versus changing, and any inspiration photos is enough to get started. For larger projects — full additions, ADUs, or anything involving structural changes — we'll usually ask to see the space in person or review existing floor plans so the rendering reflects real constraints like ceiling height, window placement, and load-bearing walls.

If you're still deciding between two or three directions for a space, that's a normal starting point — many clients request a rendering specifically to compare options side by side before settling on one.
