Paying for Your San Francisco Renovation: Protecting Your Money

By Solid Bay Construction SF | Updated 2026

How you pay for a renovation matters almost as much as what you're paying for. California law puts specific limits on down payments, and a well-structured payment schedule is one of the simplest ways to protect yourself if a project runs into trouble.

California's Down Payment Limit

Under California Business and Professions Code §7159, a licensed contractor cannot request a down payment larger than $1,000 or 10% of the contract price, whichever is less — with limited exceptions. If a contractor asks for 30% or 50% upfront on a standard remodel, that's a legal red flag worth stopping and questioning before signing anything.

What a Fair Payment Schedule Looks Like

After the limited down payment, the rest should be tied to completed, verifiable milestones — not calendar dates. A reasonable structure for a full renovation might look like:

  • Small deposit at signing (per the legal limit above)
  • Payment after demolition and rough framing is complete
  • Payment after rough electrical, plumbing, and mechanical pass inspection
  • Payment after drywall and insulation pass inspection
  • Payment after finish work (flooring, cabinetry, paint) is substantially complete
  • Final payment held until final inspection passes and a punch list is signed off

The key principle: you should never be more than one milestone "ahead" of the work actually completed. If a contractor is asking for the next payment before the current phase has passed inspection, that's worth pausing on.

Holding Back Retention

It's standard and reasonable to hold back a final 5–10% of the contract until the punch list — the small, cosmetic fixes identified at final walkthrough — is fully resolved. This gives the contractor a real incentive to finish those last details promptly rather than treating them as optional.

Protecting Yourself Further

Pay by check or bank transfer rather than cash, keep every invoice and receipt, and confirm in the contract exactly what "complete" means for each milestone before you agree to the schedule — vague milestone language is where payment disputes usually start.
