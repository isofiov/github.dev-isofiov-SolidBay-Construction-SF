What CA's Latest Building & Energy Code Means for Your Remodel

By Solid Bay Construction SF | Updated 2026

California updates its building and energy codes on a regular cycle, and each new cycle changes what's required for permitted renovation work. Here's what homeowners in San Francisco and Marin actually need to know, in plain terms.

How California's Code Cycle Works

The California Building Standards Commission publishes an updated California Building Code (based on the International Building Code) and Title 24 energy code on a roughly three-year cycle. Once a new code cycle takes effect, permit applications submitted after that date are generally reviewed against the new requirements — which is why timing a permit submission can occasionally matter for a project that's borderline on scope.

Where the Current Cycle Most Affects Renovation Projects

  • Electrification requirements: Recent energy code cycles have pushed toward electric-ready or all-electric requirements for new construction and, in some cases, major alterations — meaning gas appliance replacements increasingly require electrical circuit provisioning even when you're keeping gas for now
  • EV-ready requirements: New construction and many major renovations must now provide EV charging infrastructure (conduit and panel capacity at minimum) even if a charger isn't installed immediately
  • Insulation and envelope standards: Significant remodels that expose wall or ceiling cavities typically trigger current insulation requirements for those areas, not the standard that existed when the home was built
  • Water heating efficiency: Replacement water heaters are increasingly steered toward heat pump models under current energy code compliance paths, especially in scope-triggering renovations

What Triggers Full Code Compliance vs. Limited Scope

A key concept in California's code is that the compliance requirement generally scales with your project's scope — a small like-for-like repair usually doesn't trigger a full-house energy code upgrade, but a "substantial improvement" (commonly defined as work exceeding a percentage of the home's value, or a significant addition) can trigger broader compliance requirements for the whole structure, not just the area being worked on.

What This Means Practically

Project TypeTypical Code Impact
Single room remodel (kitchen or bath)Code applies mainly to that room's systems
Whole-house remodel or major additionCan trigger current energy code compliance for the full structure
Water heater or furnace replacementOften steered toward higher-efficiency or heat pump equipment
New construction (ADU, addition)Always built to the current code cycle in full

The specifics matter enough that it's worth having your contractor or architect confirm which requirements actually apply to your project's scope before finalizing a budget — code compliance costs are far easier to plan for upfront than to discover mid-permit-review.
