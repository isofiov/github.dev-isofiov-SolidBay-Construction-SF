Historic Preservation Rules for Victorian Renovations in San Francisco

By Solid Bay Construction SF | Updated 2026

San Francisco has one of the largest surviving collections of Victorian and Edwardian homes in the country, and the city protects them through two distinct planning codes — Article 10 and Article 11. If you own one of these homes, understanding which article applies (if either) determines how much design freedom you actually have before you start planning a renovation.

Article 10 vs. Article 11: What's the Difference?

Article 10Article 11
Applies to individually designated City LandmarksApplies to buildings rated Significant or Contributory within a designated historic district (e.g. parts of the Mission, Alamo Square, Duboce Park area)
Relatively rare — a specific, individually notable buildingFar more common — thousands of Victorians and Edwardians fall under this by virtue of their district
Review is typically stricter and can extend to interior character-defining featuresReview usually focuses on street-facing exterior alterations

How to Find Out If Your Home Is Designated

Search your address on the SF Planning Department's Property Information Map (PIM). It will show whether your building carries an Article 10 landmark designation, or an Article 11 rating (Significant, Contributory, or Unrated) within a historic district. This single lookup should be the first step before finalizing any exterior design for an older SF home — it changes your permit path entirely.

What You Can Usually Change Without Special Review

  • Interior renovations: Kitchens, bathrooms, and layout changes not visible from the street are typically reviewed under standard building code, not historic preservation review
  • Rear additions: Additions at the rear of the building, invisible from the public right-of-way, generally face less scrutiny than street-facing changes
  • Systems upgrades: Electrical, plumbing, seismic retrofit work, and HVAC replacement don't typically trigger historic review on their own

What Typically Requires a Certificate of Appropriateness

  • Replacing or altering street-facing windows, doors, or the front facade
  • Removing or altering character-defining features — original millwork, cornices, decorative brackets, or siding
  • Additions visible from the street, including many second-story additions
  • Demolition of any portion of a rated building

For Article 11 properties, this review typically happens administratively through Planning Department staff for straightforward, code-compliant work — it adds time to your permit timeline but isn't usually the multi-month ordeal homeowners fear, provided the design respects the building's original character.

Working With a Contractor Who Knows the Process

The single biggest driver of delay on a historic renovation isn't the review itself — it's submitting a design that has to be revised because it wasn't planned around historic requirements from day one. A design-build team that identifies your building's Article 10/11 status before finalizing drawings can design around the requirements rather than discovering them after a rejection.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find out if my San Francisco home is under Article 10 or Article 11?

Check the SF Planning Department's Property Information Map online, which flags individual landmarks and buildings rated Significant or Contributory within a historic district.

Can I remodel the interior of a historic Victorian without special review?

In most cases, yes. Review generally focuses on street-facing exterior changes — interiors can typically be renovated freely as long as the work doesn't affect character-defining exterior features.
