In-House Architect, Engineer & Permit Expediting: A-to-Z in San Francisco

By Solid Bay Construction SF | Updated 2026

A typical San Francisco renovation touches four separate professionals before a single wall gets opened: an architect, a structural engineer, a permit expediter, and finally the general contractor who actually builds it. Coordinating four separate firms — each with their own schedule, their own read of the budget, and no direct accountability to each other — is where most renovation timelines quietly fall apart. Here's how having all four under one roof changes that.

The Problem With the Traditional Sequence

The conventional path looks like this: hire an architect, wait for design development, hand the drawings to a structural engineer for calculations, submit to DBI (often through a separate expediter), then finally bid the project out to general contractors once permits are close. Each handoff is a place where information gets lost, and each firm's incentive is to protect their own scope — not the project's overall budget or timeline.

  • Redesign cycles: An architect's beautiful design that a structural engineer later flags as requiring an expensive moment frame, discovered only after the design is "finished"
  • Budget surprises at bid: Drawings developed without a contractor's real-time cost input often price out 20-40% over what the homeowner expected
  • Permit delays nobody owns: When plan check comments come back, the architect, engineer, and expediter each wait for someone else to respond first
  • No single point of accountability: When something goes wrong, each firm can reasonably point to another firm's scope

When Full A-to-Z Design-Build Makes the Most Sense

  • Additions and second-story build-outs: Projects where structural engineering and architectural design are tightly linked from day one
  • ADU and JADU conversions: Where Planning and DBI code triggers change the design mid-process if not caught early
  • Full-house remodels: Where dozens of small decisions across trades need one team tracking the whole picture
  • Projects on a tight timeline: Where every week saved in permit review is a week saved overall

A straightforward cosmetic remodel with no structural or layout changes doesn't need this level of coordination — but the moment a project touches a permit, a load-bearing wall, or a change of occupancy, having one accountable team stops being a convenience and starts being the thing that keeps the project on budget and on schedule.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it cheaper to hire my own architect instead of using a design-build contractor's in-house team?

Sometimes the design fee is similar either way, but hiring separately usually costs more overall once redesign cycles and sequential (rather than parallel) timelines are factored in.

What does a permit expediter actually do in San Francisco?

An expediter manages the DBI submission and plan check process — tracking comments, resubmitting quickly, and catching Planning Department triggers before they cause a rejection. Good expediting can cut plan check timelines by 30-50%.
